公冶长第五 · chapter 20Why is this “foolishness” harder to learn than cleverness?

What this chapter says

Yikonglu's reading: The “foolishness” is taking on what every clever person avoids, while still keeping himself safe and saving his lord; Master Cheng's mention of Bi Gan shows it is not simply feigning stupidity. Today: the year the company hit trouble everyone left, and she stayed to sort out the bad books. A common question is ““His foolishness cannot be matched” is used today as an insult — so why is this kind of foolishness harder to learn than cleverness” — the line above is Yikonglu's answer.

白Plain Chinese

孔子说：甯武子在国家清明时，显得聪明；国家昏乱时，显得愚拙。他的聪明，别人赶得上；他那份愚拙，别人赶不上。

A plain modern-Chinese rendering following Yikonglu's reading.

常The common reading

“His foolishness cannot be matched” is used today as an insult — so why is this kind of foolishness harder to learn than cleverness?

注Key words in the commentary

“知” is read as “zhi”, wisdom; “甯” is read nìng.

说What other commentators say

Zhu Xi: Ning Wuzi “仕卫，当文公、成公之时” — served Wei under Dukes Wen and Cheng; under Duke Wen, who had the Way, “无事可见，此其知之可及也” — there was nothing remarkable to see, so his wisdom could be matched; under Duke Cheng, who lacked the Way and lost the state, he “周旋其间，尽心竭力，不避艰险……而能卒保其身以济其君，此其愚之不可及也” — moved through it all, giving his whole heart and strength, not avoiding danger, and in the end kept himself safe and rescued his lord; that is the foolishness that cannot be matched. He cites Master Cheng: “邦无道能沉晦以免患……亦有不当愚者，比干是也” — when the state lacks the Way one may keep a low profile to avoid harm, yet there are some who should not play the fool, such as Bi Gan.
